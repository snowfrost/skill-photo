# Approved Visual Reference Guide

Use this guide together with the images in `assets/examples/positive/`. The images are the source of truth; these notes explain what to learn from them.

Use Example 02 as the canonical anchor for ordinary generation, regardless of subject category. It governs hierarchy, spatial compression, exposed paper, relief-line differentiation, and abstraction—not bridge imagery. Keep it unchanged through the complete W/L/A convergence loop. Use another single example only for an explicit exception below; never average examples together.

Interpret “woodcut” as selective relief-carved mark language inside a contemporary editorial poster. Do not interpret it as a traditional black-and-cream linocut, historical woodblock print, heritage illustration, or tourist souvenir print.

## Preset routing

- Default: **Example 02** for integrated construction across architecture, landscape, coast, mountains, people, transit, and quiet scenes.
- Choose **Example 01** for warm waterfronts, paired depths, boats, and sunset memory.
- Choose **Example 02** for stable bridges, transit, formal architecture, and restrained integrated relief printing.
- Choose **Example 03** for the clearest sticker collage, colorful facades, bright weather, and playful geometric details.
- Choose **Example 04** only for dense sunset cities, vertical towers, and deliberately integrated high-density carving.
- Choose **Example 05** only when the user explicitly prioritizes pale winter dissolution or highly minimal open space over the canonical print structure.

Treat Example 02 as the primary structural benchmark for the skill: a scene printed directly on paper, no large enclosing sticker contour, one dominant form, compressed depth, locally differentiated carving, tiny type, and one restrained color anchor. Translate these relationships across subject matter. For a mountain, the bridge's dominant arch becomes one simplified ridge or valley wall; foliage and crowds become sparse terrain rhythms; boats and bus become a few identifying cues. Never translate the sample by preserving the content photo's original camera view and merely changing it to blue engraved lines. Choose Example 03 only when the user explicitly requests sticker separation, collage, cutouts, or playful geometric fragments.

## Shared grammar

- Keep the page calm, tall, luminous, and spacious.
- Keep roughly 35%-55% as genuinely untouched paper and dense dark ink below roughly 15%-25%. This constrains ink area, never ink saturation: localized dark marks should remain firm and decisive. Never enclose the page with a carved black border or dark corner foliage.
- Use warm cream, pale oat, or cool blue-white fibrous paper without stains, burnt edges, or brown kraft heaviness.
- Build the image directly on the paper from flat relief-print inks, visible paper, imperfect registration, and controlled carved marks. Do not default to a white-bordered scenic sticker.
- Keep color limited but alive: usually two to four inks plus the paper color.
- Prefer a full-strength chromatic dark such as deep green, mineral blue, or blue-gray over dominant black. Use near-black only as a small accent. Do not interpret chromatic dark as pale, dusty, translucent, or gray-beige.
- Require a clear value ladder: untouched paper, one legible middle colored ink, one firm chromatic dark, and a small saturated counter-accent when the source supports it.
- Preserve recognizable scene anchors while simplifying secondary information into short hatching, flat blocks, interrupted contours, or paper fragments.
- Keep all text small and editorial. Never use a headline as the main object.
- Concentrate detail locally and leave large areas nearly untouched. Dense texture must still feel slow.
- Preserve unmistakable carved drawing: visible contour cuts, directional hatch groups, pressure-varied gouges, and short cross-cuts. Do not confuse paper fiber, halftone, duotone photography, distressed ink, or posterization with woodcut linework.
- Preserve only a few identifying anchors from the source. Make at least one substantial region clearly interpretive through contour bands, exposed-paper voids, repeated structural marks, ink planes, or a separate micro-fragment.
- Judge woodcut construction, structural linework, and form-plus-style abstraction separately. An image needs all three: carved material logic does not excuse weak abstraction; abundant lines do not excuse generic engraving; a small detached fragment does not excuse an intact photographic main scene.
- Distinguish true empty paper from pale imagery. If mountains, clouds, snow, or architecture remain faintly traced across an area, that area is not negative space.
- Distinguish calm from faded. Snow, fog, winter, distance, and solitude may reduce color count, but they must retain a confident dark anchor and visible secondary tone. If the whole image merges into one pale paper-adjacent value, reject it.
- Treat exhaustive rendering as drift. Reduce logos, hotel names, shop signs, window grids, leaves, and facade ornament to a few cues unless the user explicitly requests exact text or branding.

## Example 01 — river city and boat

Learn the stacked torn-paper panels, warm sunset orange against quiet blue-gray water, small captioning, and calm separation between city and foreground boat. Use for waterfronts, layered travel memories, sunset cities, and scenes with two spatial depths.

## Example 02 — bridge crossing: primary ideal

Learn the stable architectural arch, **firm saturated** dark-green linework, one unmistakable red moving accent, pale but still legible atmospheric distance, and slow horizontal river rhythm. Notice that quiet paper does not weaken the ink: the bridge separates decisively from the page, middle foliage remains visible, and the red bus stays vivid. Study how the scene has no sticker boundary or carved frame: buildings and foliage fade into untouched paper; the bridge supplies the dominant silhouette without filling the page; people, bus, boats, trees, and water are compressed into distinct rhythmic mark families. The abstraction comes from omission, hierarchy compression, flattened depth, and exposed paper—not from reduced opacity, an irregular container, faithful perspective, binary black/cream contrast, or exhaustive cutting.

## Example 03 — colored windows

Learn the clearest sticker treatment: irregular cream contours, multiple cropped fragments, partial abstraction, rainbow-like color organized into repeated architectural units, and short yellow atmosphere marks. Use for bright weather, colorful buildings, graphic facades, and playful urban detail.

## Example 04 — evening city spires

Learn the boldest interpretation: concentrated orange glow, violet-blue architectural silhouettes, selective grid dissolution, strong vertical ascent, and tiny type embedded inside the visual cluster. Use for sunset cities, towers, dramatic skylines, and denser scenes. Keep any long lines structural and inwardly organized; never turn them into explosive rays or speed effects.

## Example 05 — winter shore

Learn cool blue-white paper, a large quiet field, delicate horizontal water bands, a tiny warm human anchor, and low-contrast winter distance. Use for snow, mist, solitude, beaches, mountains across water, and intimate figures in open landscapes. Preserve enough blue-gray contrast in the middle distance to remain legible at thumbnail scale. Keep the far mountain sparse, the middle forest moderately broken, and the nearest subject or trees locally dense; do not render all three depths with one uniform fine etching. Even in pale winter scenes, keep visible contour cuts and directional hatch groups—do not replace them with blue photographic halftone. Unless collage is explicitly requested, integrate botanical, roof, branch, snow, shore, and figure cues into the same open relief field rather than separating them into bordered stickers.

## Interpretation boundary

Do not copy an example's exact composition, subject, captions, or palette. Match its relationship between paper, negative space, visible structural carving, firmly saturated colored ink blocks, open edge dissolution, abstraction, and emotional tempo. Reject seven extremes: a uniformly engraved antique illustration, a flat duotone photograph with no real carved lines, a complete photograph-shaped sticker, a large white-bordered irregular scene blob, a borderless but perspective-faithful full-scene tracing, a traditional black-and-cream linocut with carved border and dense dark perimeter, and a washed-out pale monochrome whose inks merge into the paper. Across all presets, keep relief-cut contours and directional hatching visible but localized, preserve decisive ink strength, redesign framing and scale, compress depth, and rebuild one substantial part of the main form as quiet abstract structure.
